LOCAL SUMMARY CHECKED: November 1, 2026

WHAT IS A TARMAC DELAY
A tarmac delay occurs when an aircraft is on the ground, either waiting to take off or after landing, and passengers do not have an opportunity to get off the aircraft. Waiting in a terminal is not a tarmac delay. Apply the tarmac-delay requirements only to covered flights/carriers at U.S. airports.

FOOD AND POTABLE WATER — TWO HOURS
Under 14 CFR §259.4(c)(4), during a covered tarmac delay the carrier must provide adequate food and potable water no later than two hours after the start of the tarmac delay. The pilot-in-command may withhold this service only when the pilot determines that safety or security considerations preclude it. DOT consumer guidance describes the service as a snack and drinking water; a full meal is not required. The carrier must have or obtain enough food and water to serve all passengers onboard. Do not apply the exceptions to the three-hour deplaning limit as general exceptions to this food-and-water requirement.

TOILETS, MEDICAL ATTENTION, AND CABIN TEMPERATURE
Under 14 CFR §259.4(c)(5), covered carriers must ensure operable lavatory facilities and adequate medical attention if needed during a tarmac delay. The DOT passenger guidance also lists working toilets, comfortable cabin temperatures, and adequate medical attention if needed among the protections during a tarmac delay. The carrier's contingency plan must describe how it will maintain comfortable cabin temperatures. Do not invent a blanket technical-failure exception for the toilet or cabin-temperature assurances. The specific safety/security exception for food and water is stated separately in §259.4(c)(4); the specific exceptions to the deplaning time limit are in §259.4(c)(3).

STATUS NOTIFICATION — 30 MINUTES
Under 14 CFR §259.4(c)(6), the carrier must notify passengers onboard about the status of the delay when the tarmac delay exceeds 30 minutes. Subsequent updates, including status changes, may be provided as the carrier deems appropriate; the rule does not establish a mandatory update every 30 minutes. Under §259.4(c)(7), for departing flights and diversions, whenever an opportunity to deplane exists at a suitable disembarkation point, the carrier must timely notify passengers onboard that they may deplane.

DOMESTIC THREE-HOUR LIMIT — DEPARTING FLIGHTS
Under 14 CFR §259.4(c)(1), for domestic flights a covered U.S. air carrier must provide passengers an opportunity to deplane before the tarmac delay exceeds three hours, subject to §259.4(c)(3). For a departing flight, §259.4(c)(3)(i) provides a specific way to satisfy the requirement: the flight must begin to return to a suitable disembarkation point no later than three hours after the main aircraft door is closed in order to deplane passengers. If the aircraft is in an area not under the carrier's control, the return begins when a request is made to the FAA control tower, airport authority, or other relevant authority directing the aircraft. If the aircraft is in an area under the carrier's control, the return begins when the pilot starts maneuvering the aircraft toward the suitable disembarkation point. This specific return-start provision is written for departing flights, not arriving flights.

DOMESTIC THREE-HOUR LIMIT — ARRIVING FLIGHTS
For an arriving domestic flight landing at a U.S. airport, passengers must be provided an opportunity to deplane before the tarmac delay exceeds three hours, subject to the applicable safety/security and air-traffic-control exceptions in §259.4(c)(3)(ii)-(iii). Do not state that merely beginning to move back toward a gate before three hours is sufficient for an arriving flight; the return-start language in §259.4(c)(3)(i) applies to departing flights.

EXCEPTIONS TO THE THREE-HOUR DEPLANING LIMIT
Under §259.4(c)(3)(ii), deplaning may be excepted if the pilot-in-command determines that deplaning at a suitable point would jeopardize passenger safety or security, or there is a safety-related or security-related reason the aircraft cannot leave its tarmac position to deplane. Under §259.4(c)(3)(iii), the exception also applies if air traffic control advises the pilot-in-command that returning to a suitable disembarkation point to deplane would significantly disrupt airport operations. For departing flights, the special timely return-start provision in §259.4(c)(3)(i) also applies. These are exceptions to the deplaning-time requirement; do not reuse them as blanket exceptions to food/water, lavatory, temperature, or status-notification assurances. The food-and-water safety/security exception has its own separate pilot-in-command condition in §259.4(c)(4).

APPLYING THE RULE TO A 3-HOUR-20-MINUTE SCENARIO
If passengers remain onboard after 3 hours and 20 minutes, determine whether the aircraft was departing or arriving, when the tarmac delay began, and whether one of the applicable §259.4(c)(3) exceptions applied. For a departing flight, also establish whether the aircraft began returning to a suitable disembarkation point by the three-hour limit as specified in §259.4(c)(3)(i). For an arriving flight, establish whether a safe opportunity to deplane was offered before the delay exceeded three hours, unless a qualifying safety/security or ATC exception applied. Separately check whether adequate food and potable water were made available by two hours and whether the lavatory, cabin-temperature, and notification assurances were met. A 3-hour-20-minute duration alone is not enough to determine a violation without the relevant flight stage and exception facts.

IMPORTANT DISTINCTION — TARMAC DELAY VS. TERMINAL OR GATE WAITING

A tarmac delay occurs when an aircraft is on the ground, either waiting to take off or after landing, and passengers do not have an opportunity to get off the aircraft. Waiting in the terminal or at a gate is not, by itself, a tarmac delay. The tarmac-delay time limits apply to covered situations where passengers remain onboard without an opportunity to deplane.
